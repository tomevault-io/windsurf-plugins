# Write and Humanize: Nonfiction Craft + AI Pattern Removal

This skill combines two capabilities:

- **Nonfiction Writing** — based on *On Writing Well* by William Zinsser and *Storyworthy* by Matthew Dicks
- **Humanizer** — based on Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" guide (WikiProject AI Cleanup)

Use either capability independently or together. When drafting new content, apply nonfiction craft principles. When editing existing content, apply humanizer patterns. When revising AI-assisted drafts, do both.

---

## PART ONE: NONFICTION WRITING

### Core Philosophy

Good nonfiction writing is **clear thinking made visible**, wrapped in a **story that transforms** both the teller and the reader.

- **Zinsser** teaches *how* to write: precision, clarity, simplicity, voice
- **Dicks** teaches *what* to write about and *how to shape it*: finding the moment, building narrative arc, emotional resonance

Together: **Find the transformative moment → Tell it simply and with your whole self.**

---

### Step 1: Find What You're Actually Writing About

Before writing a single sentence, identify:

**The Five-Second Moment (Dicks)**
Every great nonfiction piece centers on one small, pivotal moment that shifted something — a belief, a feeling, an understanding, an identity. Ask:
- What moment changed you, surprised you, or made the world feel different?
- What is the *smallest* version of that change?
- Realizing you were wrong. Choosing forgiveness. Feeling suddenly afraid.

> If you can't name the five-second moment, you don't yet know your story.

**The One Provocative Thought (Zinsser)**
Every successful piece should leave the reader with one thought they didn't have before. Not three insights. One. Ask:
- What is the single idea I want to plant in the reader's mind?
- What do I want them to think differently about when they finish?

**The Transformation Arc (Dicks)**
A story without change is an anecdote. Identify:
- Who is the narrator at the START?
- Who are they at the END? (Different belief, feeling, knowledge)
- Even the smallest shift counts: "I used to think X. Now I know Y."

---

### Step 2: Establish Unity Before You Write (Zinsser)

Answer these before drafting. Inconsistency in any of these kills the piece:

| Question | Choose One |
|---|---|
| **Voice** | First person (I) / Second (you) / Third (observer) |
| **Tone** | Formal / Personal-formal / Casual / Ironic / Wry / Serious |
| **Stance toward reader** | Reporter / Expert / Fellow traveler / Witness / Confessor |
| **Scope** | What single corner of the subject am I covering? |
| **Tense** | Past (story mode) / Present (immersive mode) |

Think small. Cover one corner of your subject completely and stop. The tighter the focus, the stronger the piece.

---

### Step 3: Structure the Piece

**The Lead**
The first sentence must force the reader to read the second. A great lead offers:
- Freshness — something unexpected
- Paradox or surprise — the world upended
- A question — the reader must know the answer
- A striking detail or fact — one that earns its place

Never begin with throat-clearing ("In today's world..."), a dictionary definition, or an apology.

**Starting with the End (Dicks)**
Know your five-second moment before you write word one. Then: what is the minimum backstory the reader needs to feel the weight of that moment? Everything else is cut.

**The Middle — Tension Through "But" and "Therefore"**
Weak stories use *and then... and then.* Strong stories use:
- **But** — something counters what came before
- **Therefore** — an action creates consequence

Every paragraph should either counter what preceded it (but) or build on it to create something new (therefore).

**The Ending (Zinsser)**
- Stop when the story is done. Don't summarize what you just said.
- Surprise the reader — the best endings feel inevitable yet unexpected.
- A well-chosen quotation often makes the perfect ending.
- Never end with a moral lecture or a tidy bow. Trust the story.

---

### Step 4: Write with Simplicity and Clarity (Zinsser)

**The Clutter Test** — Delete on sight:
- Adverbs propping up weak verbs: "ran very quickly" → "sprinted"
- Redundant pairs: "each and every," "past history," "end result"
- Empty phrases: "It is interesting to note that," "In order to," "Due to the fact that"
- Throat-clearing openers: "Basically," "Needless to say," "As we all know"
- Passive voice where active works: "The ball was hit by her" → "She hit the ball"

**The Conversation Test:** Would you say this to a friend? If not, rewrite it until you would.

**Use Active Verbs**
Active verbs are the muscles of prose:
- Weak: "There was a change in her expression."
- Strong: "Her expression fell."

**Word Choice**
- Common words beat fancy words
- Precise beats vague
- Concrete beats abstract: "Her hands shook" beats "She felt nervous"

**Sentence Rhythm**
Read aloud. Vary sentence length: short sentences punch; longer sentences carry the reader forward. Monotony of rhythm is the reader's enemy.

---

### Step 5: Voice — Writing as Yourself (Zinsser)

- Write in first person whenever possible. Readers connect with a human being, not a disembodied narrator.
- Relax. Stiffness is a mask. When writers tighten up trying to sound "writerly," the personality disappears.
